## Welcome

How should a financial practitioner understand an intelligent system that can retrieve evidence, take action, coordinate specialists or revise the way it works? This course approaches that question through the decisions and responsibilities of professional life. It begins with the problem to be solved, the evidence required, the authority delegated and the person or institution that remains accountable.

Across three lectures, we move from knowledge and reasoning to autonomous action, from action to institutional responsibility, and from individual systems to collective and adaptive intelligence. Technical ideas emerge through working examples. The objective is to develop the judgment needed to engage with AI specialists, assess proposals and guide responsible implementation in finance.

**Coding and advanced mathematics are not prerequisites.** Monographs, diagrams, slides, guided demonstrations and optional computational notebooks provide complementary routes into the material. There is no required textbook.

This README is the course overview and resource directory. The substantive teaching materials remain in Google Drive so that large PDFs, audio files, notebooks and source bundles can be accessed without storing them in GitHub.

## Lecture I — From Knowledge to Action

The first lecture asks what changes when a conversational model gains access to an explicit structure of knowledge, and what changes again when a system can act. A financial question rarely depends on a single isolated fact. It requires relationships among evidence, institutions, mandates and decisions. Knowledge graphs make those relationships navigable and expose the distinction between a persuasive answer and a supported conclusion.

The second transition introduces perception, judgment, execution and feedback. Driving and air-traffic control provide visible settings in which errors, constraints and supervisory decisions can be examined. They prepare the ground for the financial applications of Lecture II.

### Application A — Knowledge and reasoning

#### Example 1 — Legal knowledge graph

Legal inquiry requires more than a plausible answer. This example connects authorities, facts and relationships in a navigable knowledge graph, allowing students to examine the evidence behind a conclusion. The practical lesson is to distinguish fluent explanation from a defensible chain of reasoning, with applications to compliance, regulatory research and due diligence.

## Lecture II — From Action to Institutional Responsibility

Once a system can act, the central question becomes what it is authorised to do. Self-driving architectures extend beyond physical movement: a trading research process, an M&A team and a cyber-defence workflow can all observe, interpret, act and incorporate feedback. Their institutional consequences depend on the limits and responsibilities surrounding that loop.

The second half introduces the **AI harness**: the engineered environment of data, tools, calculations, permissions, validation, monitoring and human decision rights around a model. A strong recommendation does not automatically constitute an authorised decision. The distinction between capability and authority connects all four examples.

## Lecture III — From Individual Systems to Evolving Intelligence

The final lecture examines how intelligence can be organised across multiple agents and how that organisation can itself become adaptive. A swarm is useful when specialisation, shared evidence, independent challenge and disciplined synthesis improve the quality of a complex output. Merely multiplying agents does not establish reliability.

The legal and scientific examples deliberately contrast two forms of production: a traceable institutional draft and a falsifiable research specification. The final block then changes the object of autonomy. Systems begin to propose changes to their own procedures or working relationships, subject to evaluation and authorisation. ESCAPES brings the course together by asking how locally governed societies can collaborate without dissolving their institutional boundaries.

### Application A — Governed swarms for complex production and scientific discovery

#### Example 9 — 50 AI Agents Go to Congress

Fifty synthetic institutional specialists examine Mexico’s Securities Market Act, identify frictions, challenge proposals and assemble a preliminary twenty-article reform package. The educational value lies in the organisation of multidisciplinary drafting, adversarial review and traceable synthesis. The output remains a proposal for human legal review; the agents do not exercise legislative authority.

### Application B — Agent-designed architectures and the ESCAPES capstone

#### Example 11 — Advanced agentic architectures and self-taught optimizers

The Guide frames this example around agents that help design how their work is organised. The available materials focus on a concrete modality: the Self-Taught Optimizer (STOP). An inner loop improves candidate solutions; an outer loop evaluates and revises the procedure producing them. Model weights remain fixed. Students distinguish task utility from improver meta-utility and ask what evidence would justify changing the surrounding scaffold.

#### Example 12 — ESCAPES: an ecology of autonomous systems

The capstone begins with separate finance, engineering and cybersecurity societies, each with local expertise, memory and institutional rules. When a society reaches its competence frontier, it can seek outside expertise, form a relationship and negotiate a temporary compact. An “escape” is a bounded organisational crossing, not evasion of safety controls. The result is an ecology of locally governed societies linked by selective, accountable collaboration.

## Using the materials

Before each lecture, read the relevant essay in the collection and review its companion slides. For each example, use the monograph to understand the problem, architecture and lessons; use the deck to revisit the classroom narrative; and use the podcast, where available, as an additional route into the ideas. The notebooks expose the underlying experiments for students who wish to investigate further.

Notebook links open the original files in Drive. To run a notebook, open it with Google Colab where that option is available, or download the `.ipynb` file and upload it to Colab. Work in your own copy and follow the notebook’s setup instructions. Some experiments require model access or API credentials; these are not prerequisites for following the classroom discussion.

The resource tables link to English teaching materials and M4A podcasts, with original filenames retained for identification. Example 9 also includes both Spanish and English versions of the illustrative reform documents. All twelve examples include a monograph, slide deck, notebook and podcast.

Drive links preserve the existing sharing settings. If a file requests sign-in or access, use the account authorised for the course or request access from the owner. ## What you will take away

The intended progression is from casual use to professional and institutional proficiency. A practitioner should be able to define a problem, choose an appropriate architecture, examine evidence, evaluate results and recognise limitations. Institutional proficiency adds the ability to reason about permissions, oversight, cybersecurity, operational resilience and accountability.

The five-generations perspective provides a retrospective map of the journey, not a competing lecture structure. Familiar statistical and predictive methods form the foundation; the examples extend into knowledge-augmented intelligence, autonomous and governed systems, collective intelligence and adaptive architectures. Greater sophistication is useful only when the problem warrants it.

Throughout the course, ask:

- What does the system know, and what evidence supports its conclusions?
- How does it select actions, and what can it execute without further approval?
- How are error, disagreement and uncertainty made visible?
- What tests distinguish a compelling demonstration from an institutionally reliable process?
- Where must human judgment and responsibility remain decisive?

These are educational experiments, including exploratory prototypes. Their outputs illustrate architectures and evaluation problems; they do not by themselves establish readiness for live financial, legal or operational deployment.

The lasting outcome is a repertoire of approaches and a more disciplined question: **What form of intelligence does this problem require, how should it be governed, and what responsibilities accompany its use?**
